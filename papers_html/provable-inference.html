大语言模型可证明推理的研究进展

Progress on provable inference for large language models · Nov 2, 2026 · Frontier Red Team · 查看原文

📖 中文摘要

Anthropic发布FlashProve，用zkSNARK让用户验证回复确由被承诺模型生成，实现“可证明推理”。系统针对400B参数混合专家模型Llama 4 Maverick（4-bit量化）：单块H100生成证明约110 token/秒，每个1,024-token请求的证明约19 MB，约为vLLM运行模型GPU时间的70-95倍，远快于此前开源系统（VerInf证明同一模型1,000个token需14.3小时、证明93.6 GB）。团队用Lean形式化验证完备性、可靠性与零知识性。局限：上下文仅1,024 token、只证明贪心解码下的整数模型、隐藏权重但不隐藏架构。

专业术语注解

可证明推理（provable inference）：让用户验证某条回复确由所请求模型生成、而不必信任提供方的技术zkSNARK：零知识简洁知识论证，证明短小、校验快且不泄露模型权重FlashProve：Anthropic开源的可证明推理原型，针对MoE大模型并采用事后证明承诺（commitment）：离线阶段发布的一段简短指纹，锁定某组确切权重而权重本身保持隐藏Lean：用于规约协议并机器可检查地证明完备性、可靠性、零知识性的形式化验证语言

执行摘要

当你与朋友面对面交谈时，有很多种方式确认对方确实是本人：你能看到他们、听到他们、与他们握手。而与AI模型对话时，你几乎没有任何办法。如今，向模型发起查询的用户无法核实回复是否来自开发者所声称的那个特定模型，而非被路由服务出于成本考虑偷偷替换的模型，甚至是被篡改了token流的攻击者所替换的模型。仅凭模型的输出来判断并不可靠，即便它生成的是高质量回复。推理的密码学证明解决了这个问题：它让模型提供方先对一个模型作出承诺，再证明每条回复确由该模型生成。这类证明可以支撑从消费级产品到内部安全的各类应用，也有助于形成一套工具，用于核验是否符合AI发展节奏方面的要求。 具体而言，我们在一种强大的密码学证明上取得了进展，即"zkSNARK"，它因具备强隐藏性与高效性而格外适合可证明推理（provable inference）。在本文中，我们分享其中的部分进展，主要通过一个我们称为FlashProve的系统来呈现。简要技术总结如下： 1. Claude在面向推理的zkSNARK上做出了改进，构建出一个显著快于此前开源系统的系统。 1. 对于大型模型（Llama 4 Maverick，400B参数的混合专家模型），在4-bit量化权重、预填充阶段使用整数运算的条件下，单块H100 GPU生成证明的速度约为110 token/秒，每个1,024-token请求的证明大小约19 MB。这约为在vLLM（一个常用的开源库）上运行模型本身所需GPU时间的70–95倍。 2. 此前的开源系统要慢上几个数量级，我们在第6节中对此有讨论。Claude找到了新的方法，把密码学与安全文献中最先进的工具加以运用，同时依托密码学证明系统中久经检验的设计原则。对模型的承诺以及随后的推理证明都会隐藏模型权重；隐藏模型架构并非本系统的目标。 2. Claude擅长优化，但常常做出难以评估的决策，在大型复杂系统中尤其如此。我们的应对方式是：针对所声称的安全保证，用Lean（一种形式化验证语言）给出可审计的顶层陈述。

今天我们发布五项成果： * 本篇博文，概述我们的系统及其潜在应用； * 一份技术报告，详述我们系统的细节； * 一份（由Claude撰写的）协议规范文档； * 可执行代码，其中包括：一个证明器与两个验证器，一个用C++编写的快速验证器，以及一个用Lean编写的可证明正确的验证器；以及 * 面向顶层数学陈述的Lean证明，以及一个便于审计的Lean定理交互式可视化工具。

我们以Apache 2.0许可证发布代码、Lean证明与文档。1这篇研究文章旨在分享阶段性进展，而非一项已完成的研究议程。我们明确地公开这些成果，目的是让其他人看到我们所做的工作，拿去使用，并加以改进。

引言

如今，当用户向AI模型发送请求时，他们无法确切知道生成回复的，是否就是自己选定的那个模型。对于日常用途而言，用户主要关心输出内容，未必在意是哪个具体模型生成的，因此这个问题没那么紧迫。但在若干情形下，这种保证会很有价值。例如，在AI模型发布前后对其进行测试的独立评估方，需要确认自己正在评估的模型就是实际提供服务的模型。通过第三方路由服务使用模型的客户，希望确保token来自自己付费购买的模型。而如果网络攻击者在冒充某个模型的同时篡改了token流，用户也无法仅从输出中察觉。 然而，要实现对模型回复的验证并不容易。大多数前沿模型的权重都不公开，即便用户能够拿到权重，要核查某条回复是否由具备这些权重的模型生成，也需要实际运行该模型，而这所需的算力远超普通用户（乃至评估方）能够负担的水平。 可证明推理（provable inference，PI）的目标，是让算力有限、且对权重一无所知的用户，也能验证某条回复确实来自其所请求的模型。推动PI的动机有两方面：一是希望提升客户对AI提供方的信任；二是因为它可用于向独立评估方提供保证，也让提供方能够证明自己正在履行某些承诺与法律义务——它因而对把控AI发展前沿的节奏以及AI监管都有影响。 实现PI的一种途径，是借助一种称为_零知识简洁论证_（zero-knowledge succinct argument）的密码学对象：模型提供方在给出所声称回复的同时，附上一份简短的_证明_（比如几兆字节），用户可以_快速_验证（理想情况下在CPU上几秒内完成）。如果所声称的回复偏离了指定模型，用户的校验就会失败。重要的是，密码学技术能保证该证明不会泄露关于模型权重的_任何_信息。 今天，我们发布FlashProve：一个面向可证明推理的开源高性能原型。对于一个400B参数的混合专家模型（Llama 4 Maverick，4-bit量化），我们证明：单块H100 GPU生成证明的速度超过100 token/秒，每个1,024-token请求的证明大小约19 MB（约为vLLM——一个常用开源库——处理同等长度提示所需GPU时间的70–95倍）。 本次发布附带可执行代码、Lean形式化以及协议的技术说明。我们的目标有两个：（i）加速并便利可证明推理的发展；（ii）证明零知识简洁论证对于可证明推理是切实可行的。 我们认为，这一进展对安全、对把控AI发展节奏、对为AI客户提供完整性保证、对为审计方与监管方提供便利工具等方面，都具有重要意义。

AI普及中的信任挑战

人工智能的发展，以及它在我们的数字基础设施与经济中日益重要的角色，带来了AI提供方必须回应的信任挑战。借助_可证明推理（PI）的工具，可以更便于应对这些以及其他挑战。 PI的工具可以是硬件方案、软件方案，或两者的结合。这里我们聚焦于纯软件方案：一种称为"zkSNARK"（_零知识简洁知识论证，zero-knowledge succinct argument of knowledge）的密码学工具。通俗地说，zkSNARK是这样一种系统：它允许_证明器_（prover）凭借一份简短的"证书"，让_验证器_（verifier）相信某项给定计算被正确执行；关键在于，这份证书是_零知识的_，即除计算正确性本身所蕴含的信息外不泄露任何信息；同时它又是_简洁的_，即验证速度相比被证明的计算呈指数级加快。 下面概述若干挑战，以及zkSNARK如何帮助应对它们。

挑战1：节奏把控

一些关于监管与把控AI发展的提案，依赖于监控硬件及其使用方式。例如，可能要求提供方把硬件专门用于推理，而不是用来训练强大的新模型。提供方可能试图作弊：改用更便宜的模型提供服务，或以出人意料的方式使用算力，从而腾出资源用于训练。监管方如何能可靠地确保硬件确实用于推理？ _zkSNARK如何提供帮助：_监管方可以要求提供方提交证书，覆盖某一时段内所服务的推理请求。这些证书必须表明请求确实由所声称的模型生成。恶意提供方除非其硬件真的在服务正确的模型，否则无法满足这一要求。借助zkSNARK，监管方无需配备提供方可能拥有的复杂硬件环境，就能核查提供方的声明是否有效；与此同时，提供方也能得到保证：监管方不会获知专有的模型信息。2需要说明的是，尽管这能验证硬件正在服务推理，却不能证明硬件没有（例如）在私下同时进行训练。

挑战2：篡改检测

随着AI模型价值日增，运行它们的服务器成了首要目标。攻击者或失控的智能体可能攻陷推理服务器，窃取权重、向用户投送恶意回复（例如把恶意软件投递到用户电脑），凡此种种，同时还能混迹于真实回复之中。对手也不必来自外部。随着模型具备自我改进与自我部署的能力，一个更先进的模型可能顶替获得授权的模型运行，并把自己伪装成较弱的那个，以规避本应适用于它的分类器与监控。两种情形下的疑问是一样的：一家公司如何能确定其服务器上实际运行的究竟是_哪一组权重_？在我们的前沿安全路线图中，Anthropic设定了一个公开目标，即做出一个用于核验推理流量的原型：" 我们将在2026年9月30日前开发出可证明推理的原型，这是一种能够可靠、可证明地为AI模型输出"签名"，使其可归属于某一特定模型权重集合的技术。 " 正如我们在RSP更新中所述，我们通过一个独立但相关的项目实现了该目标，该项目为我们的推理流量签名，并在可信服务器上重算推理；而本文的工作则以不同方法追求同一目标。3 _zkSNARK如何提供帮助：_提供方可以要求服务器对每条回复都附上有效的zkSNARK。证明与对权重的一项特定承诺相绑定，因此它证明的不只是_某个_模型生成了该回复，而是_被承诺的_那个模型生成了它。攻陷服务器的攻击者，或顶替另一个模型运行的模型，都必须针对预期的权重生成有效证明才能不被发现，而它们做不到这一点。用户也可以收到这些证明，因为（由零知识性）它们不泄露任何内部信息，且（由简洁性）校验成本低廉；这限制了攻击者能通过推理API达成的破坏。

挑战3：文本溯源

人们可能希望证明自己在理解／分析某件事物上投入了大量精力。例如，开发者可能声称自己的软件库已由某个前沿网络安全模型扫描过漏洞，或者公司可能声称其商业计划得到了某个合适模型的认可。用户如何核实这类说法？用户可能没有资源自行运行该模型（也可能没有相应权限）。即便公开聊天记录也无法给出绝对保证；理论上开发者可以伪造记录，不是用前沿模型、而是用更便宜的模型或降级的版本来生成。更一般地，用户如何验证为自己提供服务的确实是他们所选定的模型？ _zkSNARK如何提供帮助：_提供方可以在每条回复之外，附上一份zkSNARK，证明该输出确实由所声称的模型在给定输入上生成。简洁性让用户无需专用硬件就能快速校验证书，零知识性则向提供方保证，用户不会获知关于专有权重的任何信息。这与核验由哪个模型生成回复的现有机制形成互补。 举例来说，在一个漏洞被不断发现的世界里，模型提供方可以在发布新代码之前，向代码开发者提供一份"失败证明"（Proof-of-Failure），证明提供方在某个特定模型上至少消耗了X个token，且并未取得某个特定结果（例如，该特定模型在尝试至少1M个token之后，仍未在某个特定补丁中发现任何缺陷）。

挑战4：模型审批

监管可能要求提供方只提供被认定安全的模型，而非那些被认为存在网络安全或安全隐患的模型。提供方可能有规避这一要求的动机，因为有风险的模型可能表现优于竞争对手，或服务成本更低。监管方如何确保只有获批模型被投入使用？ zkSNARK如何提供帮助：_监管方可以要求提供方在其回复上附加zkSNARK，以此强制使用获批模型。证明把每个请求与所用模型绑定，因此若提供方使用了未获批的模型，校验便无法通过。得益于简洁性，监管方无需重新执行每个请求，就能以轻量方式完成核查。 _关于可信硬件实现PI的一点说明。_此前的工作往往聚焦于用_可信执行环境（trusted execution environments）来实现PI。这类基于硬件的方案所提供的隐私与证明，依赖于厂商制造出能防篡改的硬件。在本研究中，我们聚焦于纯软件的密码学方案，其安全性与隐私性依赖于计算问题的困难性。即便环境（硬件、操作系统等）已被攻陷，对手若不攻破一个在计算上不可行的问题，也无法生成无效证明。可信硬件与密码学是互补的两条路径，保证与取舍各不相同。两者都将在应对上述信任挑战中发挥作用。

我们的系统：FlashProve

FlashProve是一个面向大型混合专家（Mixture-of-Experts，MoE）语言模型的可证明推理系统。更多细节见我们的技术报告；这里先概述系统。 FlashProve涉及两方：模型提供方（证明器）与用户（验证器）。它分为两个阶段： * 在离线阶段，证明器发布对模型权重的一项_承诺_，它是一段简短的指纹，用来锁定_某一组_确切的权重，而权重本身保持隐藏。后续的推理声明都将对照该承诺来核验，也就是对照本阶段所承诺的权重模型。 * 在在线阶段，当验证器发来请求（即提示词与相应的输出token）时，证明器生成一份证明文件，证明如下陈述：

被承诺的模型在求值时会生成这些输出token。 任何持有该承诺、提示词与回复的人，都能在无需权重、算力有限的条件下加以校验，并确信被承诺的权重在该提示词上运行，确实生成了这些token。

保证

一份证明文件满足以下"完备性"（completeness）与"可靠性"（soundness）性质： * 诚实的证明文件会被接受（完备性）。诚实证明器生成的证明会被验证器接受。 * 证明文件无法伪造（可靠性）。要为虚假陈述生成有效证明，实际上是不可能的（等同于攻破广为信赖的密码学假设），因此验证器无需信任证明器。

仅完备性与可靠性，一个算力强大且掌握模型权重的验证器就能实现（在提示词上运行模型，检查结果是否与回复一致）。真正让这个系统不平凡的，是下面两条性质。 * 证明文件除陈述本身外不泄露任何信息（零知识性）。验证器对权重、计算的中间值，以及每一层运行了哪些专家，都_一无所获_。模型架构（权重除外）并不隐藏。 * 证明文件小且校验成本低（简洁性）。被证明的计算，是一个400B参数（量化版本约232 GB）的模型在H100 GPU上的一次运行；而验证器收到的证明文件只有约19 MB，在24核CPU上几秒即可校验完毕。审计方，或硬件条件远逊于提供方的用户，无需接触复杂的基础设施就能校验证明文件。

评估

我们在Llama 4 Maverick（一个400B参数的混合专家模型，每个token约使用17B参数）上实测了该协议的一种实现，采用的是公开的4-bit权重。验证器的每个请求是一个1,024 token的窗口，每份证明校验一个token。证明器运行在配有单块H100与24个CPU核心的服务器上，验证器则运行在CPU上。 * 证明器。证明器运行在配有单块H100与24个CPU核心的服务器上。_离线阶段_每个模型只运行一次，耗时约39分钟。（出于我们在技术报告中讨论的技术原因，所得承诺对约1600万次请求有效，之后提供方必须重新作出承诺。）对于一批24个请求，_在线阶段_耗时约223秒，即110 token/秒，每份证明为18.78 MB。 * 验证器。验证器运行在客户端的CPU上。它无需GPU，也只能接触到对权重的一项承诺。客户端下载该承诺（总计约6.5 MB），此后每个请求下载一份证明。校验一份证明在24线程下耗时17秒（单线程下为21秒），其中已包含离线阶段的检查。

与普通推理的对比：我们在八块H100上，用同一模型的FP8版本运行了广泛使用的推理引擎vLLM，结果发现，按每块GPU计，为一批24个请求生成证明所需时间，约为vLLM一次性处理同样24个请求所需时间的72倍（多次运行介于66到77倍之间）；当vLLM以我们尝试过的最高负载运行时，这一差距约为94倍。 系统内部。FlashProve背后的底层机制，是点对点系统中部署的那一类zkSNARK（例如面向Ethereum等区块链的L2网络，如Starknet或Mantle）。我们的zkSNARK按一套成熟的配方构建：一种称为_交互式预言机证明_（interactive oracle proof，IOP）的底层信息论对象，借助哈希函数被编译成密码学证明（甚至提供后量子安全性）。我们的IOP由最先进的构件组装而成，用到了_带线性约束的码邻近性测试_与_查找论证_等工具，它们基于编码理论与复杂性理论的技术。把这些部件组合成专用于推理的定制IOP是一项精细的工作；构造的更多细节可见技术报告。

系统工程

FlashProve得益于我们做出的若干架构决策，这些决策在技术报告中有详细讨论。这里略举几例： * 证明器的工作量与实际运行过的专家成正比。在MoE模型中，一个token使用400B参数中的约17B，证明器每个token的工作量与这约17B成正比，而非与全部参数成正比（证明仍会隐藏具体选中了哪些专家）。 * 请求以批处理方式、事后证明。证明器通过一次并行遍历读取已完成的文本（类似预填充），并且每批只打开一次对权重的承诺，而非每个请求各打开一次。 * 系统设计为可装入单块GPU。每一层的模型权重被切分成若干段，一次证明一段层。权重保存在普通内存中并流式载入，因此单块80 GB的GPU配上约290 GB主机内存就足够了。

事后证明与在线证明

在把证明机制集成进推理服务器时，有一个选择：在线证明（即与推理同时进行证明）还是事后证明（即事后针对一部分token进行证明）。我们的系统采用后者。在线方案被期望做到极快，可能为效率而牺牲安全性；事后方案则允许更慢，且未必承受这种取舍。另一方面，事后证明也有局限：重算带来的额外复杂度、为流量取得保证存在延迟，以及固有的"非确定性"。我们在技术报告中详述了采用事后证明的动机与影响。

来自形式化验证的可信度

密码学代码出了名地脆弱、易出错。用户如何能信任我们今天发布的代码？ 答案是形式化验证（formal verification）：我们用Lean证明助手来规约协议及所期望的安全性质。我们以机器可检查、且保证正确（除编译器错误外）的形式来证明这些性质。我们锁定三条顶层性质： 1. 完备性（Completeness）：若陈述正确，则所生成的证明会被验证器接受。 2. 可靠性（Soundness）：任何伪造的证明都会被验证器拒绝（除非概率可忽略）。 3. 零知识性（Zero knowledge）：除验证器能从回复本身推断出的信息外，证明不泄露关于模型权重的任何信息。4

这些性质是在所谓的随机预言机模型（random oracle model）下陈述的，这是一种分析依赖哈希函数的密码学协议的信息论方法。 形式化验证留下两处重要的信任缺口。首先，形式化陈述必须经过审计，以确认它们与意图相符。这只能由人工完成（不过相比审计证明，负担要小得多）；我们提供了一个交互式可视化工具来辅助这一工作。 其次，可执行代码必须经过审计，以确认它与安全性质所针对的那个形式化定义的协议相符。如何最好地辅助这一点，取决于具体性质：因为可靠性只取决于验证器，零知识性只取决于证明器，而完备性取决于两者的交互。 * 对于可靠性，我们只需验证器代码正确即可（拥有"证明"的全部价值就在于：只要你信任验证器，就可以完全不信任证明器）。除C++验证器之外，我们还提供一个可执行的Lean验证器。Lean验证器运行更慢，但Lean能证明：它只接受我们可靠性定理中的验证器所接受的证明。尽管如此，日常使用的仍是C++验证器：它不带形式化保证，但在我们的测试中，它对每一份证明都与Lean验证器一致。 * 对于完备性，威胁模型并不假设存在对抗性攻击（证明器与验证器都是诚实的）。因此，借助Lean验证器，我们可以通过标准的软件测试来获得信心，确认诚实的证明器会生成被接受的证明：先在正确的陈述上运行证明器，再对所得证明运行Lean验证器。 * 对于零知识性，我们只需证明器正确。然而，与验证器不同，证明器是一个开销巨大的过程，运行一个Lean证明器并不现实。这里我们没有提供便利措施：我们预期，任何希望隐藏其所服务模型权重的证明器，都有足够资源进行细致的智能体和／或人工安全审计，以确保与Lean证明器等价。

相关工作

实现可证明推理，是俗称"zkML"这一研究脉络的目标。早期工作如SafetyNets（2017）验证了一个两卷积层的MNIST CNN和一个小型全连接语音模型；zkCNN（2021）在VGG-16（15M参数）上达到每份证明88.3 s；Kang et al（2022）则在全分辨率ImageNet上证明了MobileNet v2。 随后出现了Transformer的证明：ZKML的GPT-2对117M参数约需每token 159 s（≈0.006 tok/s），Modulus Labs的1.5B参数GPT-2 XL则需要90小时以上。EZKL（2023）证明一个1M参数的GPT式Transformer约需每token 966 s（≈0.001 tok/s）。性能逐步提升：zkLLM（2024）在GPU上用约15分钟证明了LLaMA-2-13B在2,048-token输入上的推理，即每个新token约0.001 tok/s，或批处理下约2.5 tok/s。2025年，zkGPT把GPT-2缩短到21.8 s（≈0.05 tok/s），zkPyTorch在单个CPU核心上以每token约150 s证明了Llama-3 8B（≈0.007 tok/s），ZKTorch用约20分钟证明了GPT-J（6B）（≈0.001 tok/s）。VerInf以≈0.02 tok/s证明了Llama 4 Maverick（正是我们所证明的同一个400B参数MoE模型）。DeepProve是当前经同行评审的最先进水平，用一次前向传播即可认证整条生成序列：它在单机上对GPT-2达到约2.9 tok/s、对Gemma 3达到1.4 tok/s，分布式下达31 tok/s。SASH的证明器（2026）在单块H100上对Llama 3.1 8B达到≈1.25 tok/s。 近期的商业努力也取得了亮眼数字，Attestable在单块H100上以53–77 tok/s证明Gemma 4 31B；截至本文写作时，Attestable尚未发布相应的开源库。 上述数字说明了过去几年可证明推理的迅猛进展。它们应当更新我们对"在零知识条件下究竟能证明什么"的先验判断。 * 2024年，zkLLM在一块A100上以约2.5 token/秒证明了一个13B模型。据我们所知，此前唯一针对Llama 4 Maverick的证明来自VerInf：它报告在DGX Spark上生成1,000个token耗时14.3小时，证明大小93.6 GB，验证几乎要花掉一整天。 * FlashProve在单块H100上以110 token/秒证明同一模型，证明仅19 MB，几秒即可验证。Attestable则独立报告对一个稠密31B模型达到53–77 token/秒。

我们相信，这一进展应能迁移到更多模型与架构上，形成一种灵活而强大的工具，其相对原生计算的开销约为2个数量级（在我们的测量中约为70到95倍），远低于此前的预期。

局限

本次发布的是一个研究原型，存在以下技术局限： * 对快速可执行代码没有形式化保证。我们提供了协议的Lean实现（包括一个可执行的"慢速"Lean验证器），以及在Lean中证明的安全性质。论文与快速可执行代码都从Lean协议派生而来，但我们不保证三者（Lean、论文、代码）彼此完全一致。 * 证明针对的是贪心解码下的整数模型。我们证明的是公开权重的一个精确整数版本，证明断言每个被校验的token都是该模型的贪心选择。来自浮点实现或采样解码的回复，可能在某个位置出现偏离，不再与被证明的计算精确吻合。一般而言，该整数版本被预期并经实测，与真实的浮点推理总体相差在某个很小的误差率之内（即便把浮点计算的非确定性考虑在内），不过这并不覆盖对抗性设计的提示词。 * 架构隐藏。我们的系统假设被服务的模型采用混合专家（MoE）架构，其中每个Transformer层的前馈块被替换为多个专家子网络，以及一个为每个token只激活少量专家的_路由器_，而我们的系统正是利用MoE结构来提升效率。其他架构（例如稠密模型）同样被广泛使用，其描述中可能包含模型提供方希望保密的知识产权。事实上，还可以设想一个进一步隐藏模型架构的系统：证明器通过隐藏承诺，同时对权重与架构（即用于推理的"可执行代码"）作出承诺，而验证器的声明则是核验该被承诺的可执行代码在被承诺模型上的执行。 * 侧信道攻击。协议目前的保证是：证明本身不泄露关于被承诺模型权重的任何信息。然而，"侧信道攻击"（例如测量生成一份证明所需的时间）仍有可能泄露关于权重的信息。我们认为这不在本次发布的范围之内。 * 更长的上下文。系统目前能证明的推理上下文最长为1,024个token。与对话场景中累积的上下文相比，这相当短。理想情况下，这一上下文长度应能扩展到前沿模型的完整上下文窗口（这类模型的上下文窗口通常达数十万乃至数百万token）。

Executive summary

When you talk to a friend in person, you have many ways to know it is really them: you can see them, hear them, shake their hand. When you talk to an AI model, you have almost none. A user querying a model today cannot check that the responses came from the specific model that a developer claims is responding, as opposed to one secretly substituted in by a routing service for cost reasons, or even by an attacker who has tampered with the token stream. Judging from the model’s output alone is unreliable, even if it produces high-quality responses. Cryptographic proofs of inference solve this problem: they let a model provider commit to a model and then prove that each response was produced by that model. Such proofs could enable applications ranging from consumer products to internal security, and could contribute to a toolkit for verifying compliance with AI pacing requirements. Specifically, we have made progress on a powerful type of cryptographic proof known as a “zkSNARK”, which is uniquely fit for provable inference (owing to its strong hiding and efficiency properties). In this post, we share some of that progress, primarily through a system we call FlashProve. A brief technical summary is as follows: 1. Claude has produced improvements on zkSNARKs for inference, building a system that is substantially faster than prior open-source systems. 1. For a large model (Llama 4 Maverick, 400B-parameter mixture of experts), a single H100 GPU generates proofs at around 110 tokens/second, with a proof size of ~19 MB per 1,024-token request, when in 4-bit quantized weights, using integer arithmetic in prefill. This is ~70-95x the GPU time of running the model itself on vLLM, a common open-source library. 2. Prior open-source systems are orders of magnitude slower, as we discuss in section 6. Claude found novel ways to apply state-of-the-art tools in the cryptography and security literature, while relying on well-established design principles for cryptographic proof systems. The commitment to the model, and subsequent inference proofs, hide the weights of the model; hiding the architecture of the model is not a goal of this system. 2. Claude is good at optimization, but often makes decisions that are hard to evaluate, especially in large complex systems. We counter this by providing top-level auditable statements in Lean (a formal verification language) for the claimed security guarantees.

Today we are releasing five artifacts: * The current blog post, which outlines our system and its potential applications; * A technical report explaining the details of our system; * A (Claude-written) protocol specification document; * Executable code that includes: a prover and two verifiers, a fast verifier in C++, and a provably correct verifier in Lean; and * Lean proofs for the top level mathematical statements, along with an interactive visualizer of the Lean theorems to aid with auditing.

We release our code, Lean proofs, and documentation under the Apache 2.0 license.1 This research post is meant to share intermediate progress, and not a completed research agenda. We are sharing this explicitly with the goal that others look at what we have done, take it, and improve it.

Introduction

When a user sends a request to an AI model, today they have no way of knowing for certain that the model they selected is the one that generated the response. For everyday uses, in which users mostly care about the output and not necessarily which specific model produced it, this is a less pressing concern. But there are several cases where such assurance would be useful. For example, independent evaluators who test AI models before and after release need to know that the model they are evaluating is the model being served. Clients who use a model through a third-party routing service want assurance that the tokens came from the model they paid for. And if a cyberattacker modifies the token stream while pretending to be a given model, a user has no way to detect it from the output alone. However, enabling verification of model responses is challenging. Most frontier models’ weights are kept private, and even if the user had access to the weights, checking that a given response was produced by a model with those weights would involve running the model, which requires computational resources far beyond what a typical user (or even evaluator) can afford. The goal of provable inference (PI) is to enable a user with limited computational resources and without any information about the weights to verify that a response came from the requested model. PI is motivated both by a desire to increase customer trust in AI providers, and—because it can be used to provide assurances to independent evaluators and allows providers to demonstrate that they are meeting certain commitments and legal obligations—it also has implications for pacing the frontier of AI development and for AI regulation. One way to implement PI is via a cryptographic object called a zero-knowledge succinct argument: the model provider sends, along with the claimed response, a short proof(say, a few megabytes) that the user can quickly verify (ideally, in seconds on a CPU). If the claimed response deviates from the specified model, the user’s check will fail. Importantly, cryptographic techniques ensure that the proof reveals nothing about the model’s weights. Today, we release FlashProve: an open-source performant prototype for provable inference. For a 400B-parameter mixture-of-experts model (Llama 4 Maverick, 4-bit quantization), we show that a single H100 GPU generates proofs at over 100 tokens/second, with proof size of ~19 MB per 1,024-token request (~70-95 times the GPU time that vLLM, a common open-source library, takes to process prompts of the same length). The release comes with executable code, Lean formalization, and a technical description of the protocol. Our aim is twofold: (i) accelerating and facilitating the development of provable inference; and (ii) demonstrating that zero-knowledge succinct arguments are practical for provable inference. We believe that this progress has meaningful implications for security, in pacing AI development , in providing AI customers with integrity guarantees , in facilitating tooling for auditors and regulators , and more.

Trust challenges in AI proliferation

The development of artificial intelligence and its growing role in our digital infrastructure and economy raises trust challenges that AI providers must answer. Addressing these and other challenges is facilitated by relying on tools for provable inference (PI). Tools for PI include hardware approaches, software approaches, or a combination of both. Here, we focus on a software-only approach: a cryptographic tool known as a “zkSNARK” (zero-knowledge succinct argument of knowledge). Informally, a zkSNARK is a system allowing a prover to convince a verifier , by way of a short “certificate”, that a given computation was executed correctly; crucially, the certificate is zero-knowledge in that it reveals no information beyond what is implied by the computation’s correctness and is succinct in that it’s exponentially faster to verify compared to the proved computation. Below we outline several challenges and how zkSNARKs help in addressing them.

Challenge 1: pacing

Some proposals to regulate and pace AI development rely on monitoring hardware and how it is used. For example, a provider might be required to devote its hardware to inference rather than to training a powerful new model. The provider might try to cheat by serving cheaper models or using compute in an unexpected way, freeing up resources for training. How can a regulator reliably ensure that the hardware is used for inference? How zkSNARKs can help: The regulator can ask the provider for certificates covering the inference requests served in a given period. The certificates must show that the requests were generated by the claimed model. A malicious provider cannot satisfy this request unless its hardware was genuinely serving the right model. Using a zkSNARK, the regulator can check that the provider claims are valid without needing the elaborate hardware setup that the provider may have, and at the same time the provider has the guarantee that the regulator does not learn proprietary model information.2 We note that although this verifies that the hardware is serving inference, it does not establish that the hardware is not, e.g., training on the side.

Challenge 2: tamper detection

As AI models grow more valuable, the servers that run them become prime targets. An attacker or rogue agent may compromise an inference server to exfiltrate the weights, serve users malicious responses (such as delivering malware to the user’s computer), and more, all while hiding among genuine responses. The adversary need not be external. As models become capable of improving and deploying themselves, a more advanced model could run in place of the one that was authorized and present itself as the weaker model, so as to avoid the classifiers and monitoring that would apply to it. In both cases the question is the same: how can a company be sure of which weights are actually running on its servers? In our Frontier Safety Roadmap, Anthropic set a public goal to produce a prototype for verifying inference traffic: “ We will develop a prototype by September 30, 2026 of provable inference, a technique for reliably, provably ‘signing’ AI model outputs in a way that makes them attributable to a specific set of model weights. ” As stated in our RSP updates, we met that goal through a separate but related project, which signs our inference traffic and recomputes inference on a trusted server; the work in this blog post pursues the same objective by a different method.3 How zkSNARKs can help: The provider can require a valid zkSNARK from the server on every response. A proof is bound to a specific commitment to the weights, so it certifies not just that some model produced the response but that the committed model did. An attacker who compromises a server, or a model that substitutes itself for another, must then produce valid proofs for the expected weights to avoid detection, which they cannot do. The user may receive the proofs as well, since they leak no internal information (by zero knowledge) and are cheap to check (by succinctness); this limits what an attacker can accomplish through the inference API.

Challenge 3: text provenance

One may want to prove that considerable effort went into understanding/analyzing something. For example, a developer might claim that their software library was scanned for vulnerabilities by a frontier cybersecurity model, or a company might claim that its business plan was given the go-ahead by an appropriate model. How can a user verify these claims? The user might not have the resources to run the model itself (or they might not have the clearance to do so). Even publishing the chat logs gives no absolute guarantees; a developer might in theory fake them, generating them not with a frontier model but instead with a cheaper one or a degraded version. In general, how can users verify that they are being served by their chosen model? How zkSNARKs can help: The provider can append, along with each response, a zkSNARK proving that the output was produced by the claimed model on the given input. Succinctness lets the user check this certificate quickly, without specialized hardware, and zero-knowledge assures the provider that the user learns nothing about the proprietary weights. This complements existing mechanisms for verifying which model produced a response. For example, in a world where exploits are being found regularly, prior to releasing new code a model provider could provide the code-developer a Proof-of-Failure that the model-provider spent at least X tokens on a specific model, and did not achieve a specific result (for example, this specific model did not find any bugs in a specific patch, after trying for at least 1M tokens).

Challenge 4: model approval

Regulation might require providers to serve only models approved as safe, rather than models deemed a cybersecurity or safety risk. A provider might have an incentive to circumvent this, since risky models might outperform competitors or be cheaper to serve. How can a regulator ensure that only approved models are served? How zkSNARKs can help: The regulator can require the provider to attach a zkSNARK to its responses, enforcing the use of approved models. The proof binds each request to the used model, so it will not verify if the provider is using an unapproved model. The regulator can check this in a lightweight way thanks to succinctness without requiring to re-execute each request. A note on PI from trusted hardware. Prior work often focuses on trusted execution environments to achieve PI. These are hardware-based solutions that provide privacy and attestation that rely on the manufacturer producing tamper-resistant hardware. In this research, our focus is on software-only cryptographic solutions whose security and privacy relies on the hardness of computational problems. Even in the face of compromised environments (hardware, operating system and more) an adversary cannot produce a invalid proof without breaking a computationally infeasible problem. Trusted hardware and cryptography are complementary approaches with different guarantees and tradeoffs. Both will play a role in addressing the above trust challenges.

Our system: FlashProve

FlashProve is a system for provable inference of large Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language models. More details are in our technical report; here we outline the system. FlashProve involves two parties: the model provider (the prover) and a user (the verifier). It is split into two phases: * In the offline phase , the prover publishes a commitment to the model weights, which is a short fingerprint to pin down one exact choice of weights while the weights themselves remain hidden. The later inference claims will be checked against that commitment, i.e., the weight model committed to in this phase. * In the online phase , upon requests from the verifier (i.e., prompts and the corresponding output tokens), the prover produces a proof file proving the following statement:

The committed model produces these output tokens when evaluated. Anyone who holds the commitment, the prompt, and the reply can check it, without the weights and with limited compute power, and be convinced that the committed weights, run on this prompt, produce exactly these tokens.

Guarantees

A proof file satisfies the following “completeness” and “soundness” properties: * A proof file is accepted if it is honest (completeness). An honest prover's proof is accepted by the verifier. * A proof file cannot be forged (soundness). It is effectively impossible (i.e., tantamount to breaking widely believed cryptographic assumptions) to produce a valid proof of a false statement, so the verifier does not have to trust the prover.

Completeness and soundness alone can be achieved by a computationally powerful verifier that knows the model weights (evaluate the model on the prompt and check whether the result matches the response). The next two properties are what makes the system non-trivial. * A proof file reveals nothing beyond the statement (zero-knowledge). The verifier learns nothing about the weights, the intermediate values of the computation, or which experts ran at every layer. The model architecture (excluding the weights) is not hidden. * A proof file is small and cheap to check (succinctness). The attested computation is a run of a 400B-parameter (about 232 GB for a quantized version) model on an H100 GPU, while the verifier receives a proof file of about 19 MB that takes seconds to check on a 24-core CPU. An auditor, or a user with far less hardware than the provider, can check proof files without access to elaborate infrastructure.

Evaluation

We measured an implementation of the protocol on Llama 4 Maverick (a 400B-parameter mixture of experts, with ~17B parameters used per token), using the publicly available 4-bit weights. Each verifier request is a window of 1,024 tokens, and each proof checks one token. The prover runs on a server with a single H100 and 24 CPU cores, and the verifier on a CPU. * Prover. The prover runs on a server with a single H100 and 24 CPU cores. The offline phase runs once per model and takes about 39 minutes. (For technical reasons we discuss in the technical report, the resulting commitment is good for about 16 million requests, after which the provider has to commit again.) The online phase , on a batch of 24 requests, takes the prover about 223 seconds, which is 110 tokens per second, and each proof is 18.78 MB. * Verifier. The verifier runs on the client's CPU. It needs no GPU and only has access to a commitment to the weights. The client downloads the commitment, ~6.5 MB in all, and then one proof per request. Checking a proof takes 17 seconds on 24 threads (and 21 seconds on a single thread), including the offline phase's checks.

Comparison with plain inference: We ran vLLM, a widely used inference engine, with the FP8 release of the same model on eight H100s, and found that, per GPU, proving a batch of 24 requests takes about 72 times longer than vLLM takes to process the same 24 requests at once (66 to 77 across runs), and about 94 times longer when vLLM runs at the highest load we tried. Inside the system. The underlying mechanism behind FlashProve is a zkSNARK of the kind deployed in peer-to-peer systems (e.g., L2 networks such as Starknet or Mantle for blockchains like Ethereum). Our zkSNARK is built according to a well-established recipe: an underlying information-theoretic object called an interactive oracle proof (IOP) is compiled __ into a cryptographic proof by relying on hash functions (and offers even post-quantum security). Our IOP is assembled from state-of-the-art building blocks using tools such as code proximity tests with linear constraints and lookup arguments , which are based on techniques from coding theory and complexity theory. Combining these pieces into a bespoke IOP for inference is a delicate task; more details about the construction can be found in the technical report.

Engineering the system

FlashProve benefits from architectural decisions that we took, and which are discussed in detail in the technical report. Here we highlight a few: * The prover's work is proportional to the experts that actually ran. In a MoE model a token uses ~17B of the 400B parameters, and the prover's work per token is proportional to those ~17B instead of all parameters (the proof still hides which experts were chosen). * Requests are proved in batches, after the fact. The prover reads finished text in one parallel pass (as in prefill), and it opens the commitment to the weights once per batch instead of once per request. * The system is designed to fit on a single GPU. The model weights at each layer are split to segments and are proven one segment of layers at a time. The weights are kept in ordinary memory and streamed in, so a single 80 GB GPU, with ~290 GB of host memory beside it, suffices.

Post-hoc versus in-line

When integrating proving mechanisms into inference servers there is a choice: in-line proving (i.e. proving alongside the inference) versus post-hoc proving (i.e. proving after-the-fact, for a subset of tokens). Our system follows the latter. In-line solutions are expected to be ultra-fast and may sacrifice security for efficiency, whereas post-hoc solutions are allowed to be slower and may not suffer such a tradeoff. On the other hand, post-hoc proving has limitations: added complexity of recomputation, the delay in getting guarantees about your traffic, and inherent “non-determinism”. We elaborate on the motivation and implications of post-hoc proving in the technical report.

Trustworthiness from formal verification

Cryptographic code is notoriously brittle and error-prone. How can users trust the code we’re releasing today? The answer is formal verification : we use the Lean proof assistant to specify the protocol and the desired security properties. We prove these properties in a form that is machine-checkable and guaranteed to be correct (up to compiler errors). We target three top-level properties: 1. Completeness : if the statement is correct, the resulting proof is accepted by the verifier. 2. Soundness : any fake proof is rejected by the verifier (except with negligible probability). 3. Zero knowledge : the proof reveals no information about the weights of the model, beyond what the verifier can infer from the response itself.4

These properties are stated in the so-called random oracle model, an information-theoretic way to analyze cryptographic protocols that rely on hash functions. Formal verification leaves two important trust gaps. First, the formal statements must be audited to ensure that they match their intent. This has to be done manually (though it is dramatically less onerous than auditing proofs); we provide an interactive visualizer to assist with this. Second, the executable code must be audited to ensure it matches the formally-defined protocol for which the security properties are proven. How to best assist with this depends on the property, because soundness depends solely on the verifier, zero-knowledge depends solely on the prover, and completeness depends on their interaction. * For soundness, we only need the verifier code to be correct (the entire value of having “proofs” is that you can fully distrust the prover if you trust the verifier). We provide an executable Lean verifier, alongside the C++ verifier. The Lean verifier runs more slowly, but Lean proves that it accepts only what the verifier in our soundness theorem accepts. Nevertheless, the C++ verifier is the one to use day to day: it carries no formal guarantee, and it agrees with the Lean verifier on every proof in our tests. * For completeness, the threat model does not assume adversarial attacks (the prover and verifier are honest). Therefore, a Lean verifier lets us gain confidence through standard software testing that the honest prover produces accepting proofs: run the prover on a correct statement, and then the Lean verifier on the resulting proof. * For zero knowledge, we only need the prover to be correct. However, unlike the verifier, the prover is an expensive procedure, and it would be impractical to run a Lean prover. We do not provide facilitations here: we expect that any prover that wants to hide the weights of a model they are serving has access to enough resources for careful agentic and/or human security audits to ensure equivalence to the Lean prover.

Prior work

Achieving provable inference is the objective of a line of work colloquially termed “zkML”. Early works, such as SafetyNets (2017) verified a two-conv-layer MNIST CNN and a small fully connected speech model, zkCNN (2021) reached VGG-16 (15M parameters) at 88.3 s per proof, and Kang et al. (2022) proved MobileNet v2 on full-resolution ImageNet. Transformer proofs followed: ZKML’s GPT-2 took about 159 s per token for 117M parameters (≈0.006 tok/s), and Modulus Labs’ 1.5B-parameter GPT-2 XL needed over 90 hours. EZKL (2023) proved a 1M-parameter GPT-style transformer in about 966 s per token (≈0.001 tok/s). Performance progressively improved: zkLLM (2024) proved LLaMA-2-13B on a 2,048-token input in about 15 minutes on GPU, which is ≈0.001 tok/s per new token, or ~2.5 tok/s in the batch. In 2025, zkGPT reduced GPT-2 to 21.8 s (≈0.05 tok/s), zkPyTorch proved Llama-3 8B at ~150 s per token on one CPU core (≈0.007 tok/s), and ZKTorch proved GPT-J (6B) in about 20 minutes (≈0.001 tok/s). VerInf proved Llama 4 Maverick (the same 400B-parameter MoE model we prove) at ≈0.02 tok/s. DeepProve, a current peer-reviewed state of the art, certifies an entire generated sequence with a single forward pass. It reaches about 2.9 tok/s for GPT-2 and 1.4 tok/s for Gemma 3 on one machine, and 31 tok/s distributed. SASH's prover (2026) reached ≈1.25 tok/s on Llama 3.1 8B on a single H100. Recently commercial efforts have reached impressive numbers, with Attestable proving Gemma 4 31B at 53–77 tok/s on a single H100; at the time of writing, Attestable has not released a corresponding open source library. The above numbers speak to the precipitous progress of provable inference in the past years. They should update our priors on what it is possible to prove in zero-knowledge. * In 2024, zkLLM proved a 13B model at about 2.5 tokens/second on one A100. The only earlier proof of Llama 4 Maverick that we know of, VerInf, reports 14.3 hours for 1,000 tokens on a DGX Spark, with a 93.6 GB proof which took almost a day to verify. * FlashProve proves the same model at 110 tokens/second on a single H100, with 19 MB proofs that can be verified in seconds. Attestable independently reports 53–77 tokens/second for a dense 31B model.

We believe that the progress should transfer to more models and architectures, yielding a flexible and powerful tool with overhead over native computation of roughly 2 orders of magnitude (about 70 to 95 times in our measurements), much less than previously expected.

Limitations

This release is a research prototype, and has the following technical limitations: * No formal guarantees on fast executable code. We provide a Lean implementation of the protocol (including an executable “slow” Lean verifier), along with security properties proved in Lean. The paper and the fast executable code are derived from the Lean protocol, but we do not guarantee that all three (Lean, paper, code) match each other. * The proof is about an integer model under greedy decoding. We prove an exact integer version of the published weights, and a proof asserts that each checked token is that model’s greedy choice. A reply from a floating-point implementation, or sampled decoding, could depart at some position and no longer exactly match the computation proved. The integer version is generally expected and empirically tested to be within some small error rate of true floating-point inference generically (even taking into account non-determinism of floating-point computations), though this does not cover adversarially designed prompts. * Architecture hiding. Our system assumes the served model uses a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture, in which each transformer layer’s feed-forward block is replaced by multiple expert sub-networks and a router that activates only a small subset of experts for each token and our system uses the MoE structure for efficiency gains. Other architectures, such as the dense models, are also widely used and its description may contain intellectual-property the model provider would like to keep secret. Indeed, one could also imagine a system that further hides the architecture of the model: the prover commits to both the weights and the architecture (i.e., the “executable code” for inference) via a hiding commitment and the verifier’s claim is about checking the execution of that committed executable on the commitment model. * Side channel attacks. The current guarantee of the protocol is that the proof itself reveals nothing about the weights of the committed model. However, “side channel attacks”, such as measuring the time it takes to produce a proof, could potentially reveal information about the weights. We consider this out of scope for this release. * Longer contexts. The system currently proves inference over contexts of up to 1,024 tokens. This is quite short in comparison to the contexts accumulated in chat settings. Ideally, this context length could be increased to full context windows for frontier models (which regularly have context windows of 100s of thousands to millions of tokens).
